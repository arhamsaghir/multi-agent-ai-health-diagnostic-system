Chapter 1
Introduction
1.1
Background and Motivation
Technical interviews are an important part of the modern recruitment, as they assist organiza-
tions to assess skills of a candidate, his/her reasoning ability, and professional preparedness.
Nevertheless, the conventional face-to-face or online interviewing has a number of limita-
tions that include cross-time zone coordination, interviewer bias, time wastage, as well as
lack of scaling ability in the rush periods of hiring. Such problems tend to slow the recruit-
ment process and affect it negatively to both candidates and companies negatively [?].
The latest developments in conversational AI provide possible solutions. The current
systems that rely on speech-to-text (STT), large language models (LLM), and text-to-speech
(TTS) can automate a number of aspects of an interview process. Nonetheless, the majority
of the applications available are still restricted to mere chats or one-sided voice. They are not
able to use a structured, multi-phase interview combining dialogues, behavioral questions,
and coding activities. The necessity of this gap is the emergence of a rapid, AI-powered
interview assistant that will be able to perform coherent, real-time interview processes with
both voice and interactive elements.
Figure 1.1 illustrates this workflow clearly, showing how a candidate interacts through
audio input, the system processes it via STT and LLMs, and generates spoken output through
TTS, forming a complete conversational loop.

1.2
Problem Statement
Current automated interview tools are reactive rather than proactive. They can handle iso-
lated responses but cannot manage full, multi-stage interview processes. The major limita-
tions identified in the project PPT include the following [1]:

• High Latency: Traditional systems process STT, LLM inference, and TTS sequen-
tially, creating pauses that disrupt natural conversation.
• Lack of State Tracking: Most agents do not track interview stages, resulting in dif-
ficulties transitioning between resume discussion, behavioral questions, and coding
tasks.
• Hybrid Input Challenges: Managing both voice conversation and live coding input
simultaneously remains a technical challenge.
In addition to the above limitations, current systems also struggle with maintaining con-
textual continuity across interactions. Since these tools do not retain a structured understand-
ing of the interview flow, they often generate responses that are either repetitive or misaligned
with the intended stage of the interview. This reduces the overall effectiveness of the system
and negatively impacts the candidate experience.
Another significant issue is the lack of standardized evaluation mechanisms. Most auto-
mated tools focus only on generating questions and capturing responses, but they do not pro-
vide reliable methods to assess candidate performance in a consistent and objective manner.
This leads to variability in evaluation and limits the usefulness of such systems in real-world
recruitment scenarios.
Furthermore, existing systems are not designed to dynamically adapt to candidate re-
sponses. In a real interview, the difficulty level and direction of questions often change
based on the candidate’s performance. However, most automated tools lack this adaptability,
resulting in rigid and less engaging interactions.
These gaps make it difficult to build a truly autonomous interview assistant. Human
intervention is still required in most systems to maintain flow, select questions, and evaluate
candidate performance—limiting scalability and consistency. As a result, organizations are
unable to fully leverage automation in their hiring processes, and the benefits of AI remain
only partially realized.
Therefore, there is a clear need for a more advanced system that can overcome these
limitations by maintaining interview state, ensuring low latency, supporting multimodal in-
teraction, and providing structured evaluation. Such a system would enable seamless, scal-
able, and consistent interview experiences, bridging the gap between traditional interviewing
methods and AI-driven automation.

1.4
Novelty and Originality
The standout originality of the project is encapsulated in the AI-driven interview state
machine. The key difference is that the system does not only react to questions but actively
guides the user through the interview process. The key outputs of the LLM are structured
JSONs that contain:
• the next interview question,
• the next state (e.g., BEHAVIORAL_QUESTIONS),
• evaluation notes.
This transforms the system from a reactive chatbot into a structured interview conductor.
Unlike traditional systems that rely on linear or stateless interactions, the proposed approach
maintains a dynamic representation of the interview flow, enabling smooth transitions be-
tween different stages. This ensures that the interaction remains coherent, context-aware,
and aligned with real-world interview practices.

Literature Review
2.1
Historical Overview
This is a change in the way AI is used to recruit employees during the last 10 years. Early
chatbot systems, including ELIZA and workflow assistants based on rules, were based on
predefined scripts. was not able to accommodate dynamic or multi-stage processes to the
interview processes [3]. These systems provided only simple question and answer dialogues
and no conversational memory or contextual. depth.
These early systems were limited in their ability to understand user intent and lacked
adaptability. Since they operated on fixed rule-based logic, they were unable to respond
effectively to unexpected inputs or variations in user behavior. As a result, their application
in complex domains such as recruitment and interviewing remained minimal.
Conversational agents were also able to process multi-turn interactions through the ad-
vent of deep learning methods, particularly RNNs and LSTMs [4]. Yet, these models also
failed in processing long-context conversations, understanding speech, and producing struc-
tured responses.
Although RNNs and LSTMs represented a significant advancement over rule-based sys-
tems, they still suffered from issues such as vanishing gradients and limited memory capacity,
which restricted their ability to retain long-term dependencies in conversations. This made
it challenging to maintain coherence in extended dialogues, which is essential in interview
scenarios.
Furthermore, these models primarily focused on text-based interaction and lacked seam-
less integration with speech technologies such as Speech-to-Text (STT) and Text-to-Speech
(TTS). As a result, they were not well-suited for real-time, voice-driven applications required
in modern interview systems.
The emergence of transformer-based architectures and large language models (LLMs)
has significantly advanced the field of conversational AI. These models are capable of un-
derstanding context over long sequences, generating coherent responses, and adapting to
diverse conversational scenarios. This evolution has paved the way for more sophisticated
applications, including AI-driven interview systems that can simulate structured, multi-stage
interactions with high accuracy and realism.

2.2
Recent and State-of-the-Art Work
The actual breakthrough for conversational AI was achieved with the transformer-based large
language models like GPT and Llama, which have shown high levels of comprehension,
generation, and ability to process long interview dialogues [2]. These models leverage self-
attention mechanisms that allow them to capture long-range dependencies within text, mak-
ing them highly effective for maintaining contextual continuity in extended conversations
such as interviews.
The recent literature also shows the increasing role of AI in recruitment processes like re-
sume analysis, skill assessment, and candidate screening. Nevertheless, most of the existing
solutions are not capable of conducting multi-stage interviews. They often focus on isolated
tasks rather than providing a unified system that can manage the complete interview life-
cycle, including progression across different stages and contextual evaluation of candidate
responses.
The recent automated interviews are based on a technological stack consisting of three
key technologies [1]:
Speech-to-Text (STT)
The recent automated interviews like Deepgram and AssemblyAI are based on transformer-
based acoustic models to achieve near-human-level accuracy for speech-to-text tasks. The
recent automated interviews have also shown their ability to process speech in real time,
which is a prerequisite for conversational AI.
In addition, modern STT systems are designed to handle diverse accents, background
noise, and variations in speaking styles, improving robustness in real-world environments.
This is particularly important in interview scenarios, where candidates may have different
speech patterns and varying levels of clarity.
Large Language Models (LLMs)
The recent automated interviews are based on the cognitive ability of the system. The recent
breakthroughs include:
• Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) to ground responses in external data sources.
• Function calling and JSON-based structured output for integrating external tools.
• Improved long-context handling for extended conversations.
8

Structured output is particularly relevant to automated interviews since it enables the
model to define the next interview state. It allows the system to move beyond simple conver-
sational responses and incorporate decision-making capabilities, such as selecting appropri-
ate questions and managing interview progression.
Furthermore, LLMs enable adaptive questioning strategies, where the system can mod-
ify the difficulty and direction of questions based on candidate responses. This enhances
personalization and creates a more engaging and effective interview experience.
Text-to-Speech (TTS)
The Neural TTS technologies like WaveNet and ElevenLabs have made it possible to synthe-
size speech realistically. This makes the speech of the AI interviewer sound natural, thereby
increasing candidate engagement during automated interviews.
Recent advancements in TTS have also introduced improvements in prosody, emotional
tone, and voice customization, allowing systems to generate speech that closely resembles
human conversation. This contributes significantly to user experience by making interactions
more natural and less mechanical.
Overall, the integration of STT, LLMs, and TTS forms the backbone of modern conversa-
tional AI systems. However, despite these advancements, there remains a gap in combining
these technologies into a unified, low-latency, and state-aware system capable of conducting
fully structured, multi-stage interviews.

2.4
Identification of Research Gap
Despite the advancements made in technology, some gaps exist in the current automated
interviews. While recent developments in conversational AI have significantly improved
individual components such as speech recognition, language understanding, and speech syn-
thesis, the integration of these components into a cohesive and intelligent interview system
remains a challenge.
1. Lack of Multi-Stage Interview Orchestration
The existing systems are best suited for one-on-one conversations but are not capa-
ble of managing the transition between structured interviews for résumé discussions,
behavioral interviews, technical questions, or coding evaluation. Without a state con-
troller, the interviews are unstructured and unpredictable.
In addition, the absence of a well-defined interview flow leads to inconsistencies in
candidate evaluation. Systems often fail to ensure that all candidates are assessed un-
der similar conditions, which affects fairness and reliability. A structured orchestration
10


--- Page 21 ---
Automated Interview System
AKGEC, Ghaziabad
mechanism is therefore essential to maintain coherence and standardization across dif-
ferent interview stages.
2. Insufficient Guiding Logic
Most of the existing interview agents based on LLMs are not proactive; they only react
to the input provided to them. There is no mechanism to predict the next stage of the
interview, evaluate the response of the candidate, or transition between stages on their
own.
Furthermore, these systems lack decision-making capabilities required to simulate real
interview behavior. In a human-led interview, the interviewer dynamically adjusts
questions based on candidate performance, but current systems fail to replicate this
adaptability. This limitation reduces the effectiveness of automated interviews and
makes interactions less engaging.
3. Lack of End-to-End Latency Analysis
Though each of these individual components, namely STT, LLM Inference, TTS, etc.,
is efficient in its own right, not much literature is devoted to optimizing the overall
conversational process. The overall ‘glass-to-glass’ latency, i.e., the time between
the termination of speech input by the user and the initiation of speech output by the
system, is not optimized in existing literature.
This lack of optimization results in noticeable delays during interaction, which disrupts
the natural flow of conversation. In real-world interview scenarios, even small delays
can affect user experience and reduce the perceived intelligence of the system. There-
fore, minimizing end-to-end latency is a critical requirement for achieving seamless
real-time communication.
The proposed project attempts to fill these gaps by designing a system for a voice-based
automated interview system that incorporates these technologies in a state machine frame-
work. By introducing structured state management, proactive decision-making, and opti-
mized real-time communication, the system aims to provide a more coherent, scalable, and
efficient solution for automated interviewing.

The methodology adopted for the project is a pragmatic experiment-based research method-

3. Integration: Linking all components into a single, low-latency conversational loop.

3.5
Evaluation Metrics
The metrics for evaluating the proposed system are as follows:
• Glass-to-Glass Latency: The time taken from when a candidate ends speaking until
the system starts its response. The goal is to attain a Glass-to-Glass Latency of less
than < 800 ms.
This metric represents the overall responsiveness of the system and is critical for main-
taining a natural conversational flow. It includes delays introduced by speech recog-
nition, LLM processing, and speech synthesis. Lower latency ensures a smoother
interaction experience and improves user perception of system intelligence.
• Word Error Rate (WER):The accuracy of the speech transcription results obtained
from the STT engine. The goal is to attain a Word Error Rate of less than < 10%.
WER is a standard metric used to evaluate the performance of speech recognition
systems. It measures the percentage of errors in transcription, including substitutions,
insertions, and deletions. A lower WER indicates higher transcription accuracy, which
is essential for ensuring that the LLM receives correct input for further processing.
• State Transition Accuracy: The percentage of times the system accurately predicts
the next interview stage based on the candidates’ responses. The goal is to attain a
State Transition Accuracy of more than > 95%.
This metric evaluates the effectiveness of the state machine and the decision-making
capability of the LLM. High state transition accuracy ensures that the interview pro-
gresses logically and consistently, closely mimicking the behavior of a human inter-
viewer.
• Task Completion Rate: Percentage of coding tasks successfully completed within the
expected constraints. Target: > 90%.
This metric reflects the system’s ability to present clear and solvable coding tasks
while accurately evaluating candidate responses. A high task completion rate indicates
that the system provides appropriate difficulty levels and maintains clarity in problem
statements.
In addition to these primary metrics, the system can also be evaluated qualitatively based
on user experience, naturalness of interaction, and overall satisfaction of candidates. These
factors, although subjective, play an important role in determining the practical usability and
acceptance of the system in real-world scenarios.

4.1
System Architecture Overview
The system presented herein, referred to as Julius AI, is an artificial intelligence-based in-
terview system designed to enable realistic interviews via real-time voice interaction, coding
challenges, and assessment. The system is based on a distributed client-server design that
incorporates real-time speech processing, reasoning via language modeling, and voice syn-
thesis.
The architecture is designed to ensure scalability, modularity, and efficient handling of
real-time interactions. Each component of the system operates independently while remain-
ing tightly integrated through well-defined communication protocols. This modular design
allows for easier maintenance, testing, and future enhancements.
The frontend is developed using Next.js and React technologies to provide an interactive
interface to the candidates to express the spoken input, write the code, and get the response
from the AI interviewer. The backend is developed using Node.js technology along with
WebSockets to provide real-time communication.
The frontend is responsible for managing user interaction, including capturing audio
input, displaying real-time feedback, and providing a coding interface for technical tasks.
It ensures a smooth and intuitive user experience by offering clear visual indicators of the
interview state and seamless transitions between different stages.
The speech-to-text processing is carried out by Deepgram, which transcribes the user’s
speech in real-time. The transcription is then sent to the orchestrator, which controls different
stages of the interview and sends the message to the correct AI agent, who uses a large
language model via the Groq API for contextual interview responses.
The orchestrator acts as the central control unit of the system, coordinating communica-
tion between different components. It ensures that the correct context is maintained and that
the interview progresses logically according to predefined states. This layer plays a crucial
role in maintaining consistency and coherence throughout the interaction.
The response provided by the system is then converted to spoken words using ElevenLabs
text-to-speech services. The audio is then streamed to the client. Data related to the session,
such as the conversation history and the transcript, is stored in Redis to make the session
persistent.
The use of Redis enables fast data retrieval and efficient session management, ensuring
that the system can handle multiple concurrent users without performance degradation. It

Key Algorithms
The system relies on a number of key algorithms to enable the efficiency of the interviews.
These algorithms are designed to ensure synchronization between different input modali-
ties, optimize resource utilization, and maintain a natural conversational flow throughout the
interview process.
Dual-Stream Invocation Algorithm
The most fundamental algorithm is related to the synchronization of spoken input with
code editing. The system is designed to monitor two streams:
• Speech transcripts from the STT engine
• Code keystrokes from the coding editor
The AI agent is invoked only when one of the following conditions is satisfied:
• The candidate submits code explicitly.
• Both speech and code input streams become idle.
• Speech input stops and no code activity is detected.
This strategy prevents unnecessary model invocation and ensures that the AI interviewer
responds only when the candidate has finished expressing their thoughts.
In addition, the algorithm incorporates timing thresholds and activity detection mecha-
nisms to accurately determine user intent. By monitoring pauses in both speech and typing
activity, the system avoids interrupting the candidate during active problem-solving. This
leads to a more natural and less intrusive interaction.
The dual-stream approach also improves system efficiency by reducing redundant calls
to the LLM, thereby conserving computational resources and lowering operational costs.
Furthermore, it enhances response relevance, as the model processes more complete and
context-rich inputs rather than partial or fragmented data.
Overall, this algorithm plays a critical role in balancing responsiveness with user expe-
rience, ensuring that the system behaves in a manner similar to a human interviewer who
listens attentively before responding.
