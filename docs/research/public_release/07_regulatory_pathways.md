# 07 — Regulatory Pathways for Public Release of a Consumer Symptom-Checker (India / US / EU), October 2026

**Project:** AegisMed — multi-agent symptom-based medical diagnosis system (FastAPI + Next.js; SQLite session store; FHIR R4 in/out; condition likelihoods + severity + recommendations from free-text symptoms).
**Purpose:** Establish the regulatory and legal reality of releasing AegisMed to the general public, with India as the primary market and US/EU as secondary.
**Compiled:** 28 October 2026. Evidence rules: primary regulatory sources fetched verbatim where possible; every load-bearing claim is supported by ≥3 independent sources or flagged **WEAK-EVIDENCE**; gaps are marked **UNKNOWN**.

---

## TL;DR

1. **AegisMed is a medical device in India.** Under the Medical Device Rules (MDR) 2017, "software" is expressly includable in the device definition, and CDSCO's 2025–2026 Medical Device Software (MDSW) guidance — built on the IMDRF SaMD framework — treats software that "triages patients, provides a definite diagnosis or suggests likely diagnosis for further confirmation by physician" as a regulated MDSW [1][2][3]. A consumer symptom-checker outputting condition likelihoods for free-text symptoms is most plausibly **Class B or C** (and potentially **Class D**, because CDSCO's table says SaMD used by *non-clinical users* in a "serious situation or condition" may be treated as used in a "critical situation or condition") [1].
2. **India has no "wellness carve-out" for diagnostic software.** The practical escape hatches are (a) narrowing the *intended purpose* to general health information (still legally risky), or (b) registering as a device — Class A/B licensing runs through the State Licensing Authority on the MD Online portal (Form MD-3 → MD-5; ~180-day statutory clock; ₹500/device + ₹5,000/₹10,000 premise fee) [1][30][31].
3. **India's Telemedicine Practice Guidelines 2020 prohibit AI from counselling or prescribing** — "Only a Registered Medical Practitioner may" [5][6][7]. AegisMed must never present itself as, or be marketed as, a teleconsultation substitute; AI decision-support that *assists* an RMP is permitted, independent AI counselling is not.
4. **DPDP Act 2023 + DPDP Rules 2025 (notified 13–14 November 2025) apply fully.** Consent must be "free, specific, informed, unconditional and unambiguous with a clear affirmative action" [8]; children (under 18) need verifiable parental consent [8][9]; penalties reach **₹250 crore** (security-safeguard failure) and **₹200 crore** (children's-data violations) [9][10][11]. Full compliance deadline ≈ **13 May 2027**; consent-manager regime from ≈ 13 November 2026 [12][13].
5. **US:** FDA's CDS guidance (final Sept 28 2022; **revised final January 2026** [14][15]) interprets FD&C Act §520(o)(1)(E) [16]. All four non-device criteria are *clinician-facing* ("recommendations **to a health care professional**… enabling such health care professional to independently review the basis"). A **consumer** symptom-checker does not fit the exclusion; FDA's §520(o)(1)(B) "healthy lifestyle" exclusion requires the function to be "unrelated to the diagnosis, cure, mitigation, prevention, or treatment of a disease or condition" [16]. FDA's Jan-2026 guidance treats CDS for time-critical conditions (sepsis, stroke — action within ~24h) as device-regulated [15][17]. Enforcement (warning letters) targets unapproved/misbranded diagnostic claims [18][19].
6. **EU:** MDR 2017/745 Annex VIII Rule 11 puts software providing information "used to take decisions with diagnosis or therapeutic purposes" at **Class IIa+** [20]; MDCG 2021-24's own example — "MDSW intended for diagnosing depression based on a score resulting from inputted data on patient symptoms (e.g. anxiety, sleep patterns, stress etc.)" — is **Class IIa** [20]. CE marking requires a Notified Body, ISO 13485 QMS and clinical evaluation (Ada Health certified Dec 2022; Infermedica reached Class IIb in Sept 2025) [28][29]. The **EU AI Act** makes MDR Class IIa+ AI systems high-risk via Article 6(1)/Annex I; the AI Omnibus (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, in force 27 July 2026) deferred those obligations to **2 August 2028**, while transparency duties (Art. 50) apply from 2 August 2026 [24][25][26].
7. **Cross-cutting:** WHO's AI-for-health guidance (2021; LMM guidance Jan 2024) sets the soft-law benchmark: human autonomy, transparency, accountability, inclusiveness, sustainability [21][22][23]. Liability is shifting toward developers — *Raine v. OpenAI* (2025–26) tests strict product liability for chatbot health advice [35][36]. Apple 1.4.1/1.4.2 and Google Play's Health apps policy both require regulatory proof, disclaimers and "consult a doctor" reminders [32][33]. India's Drugs and Magic Remedies Act 1954 criminalises misleading health advertising (up to 1 year imprisonment) [34].

## 1. India (primary market)

### 1.1 Is a symptom-checker SaMD under the MDR 2017?

**Yes, on its face.** The MDR 2017 device definition expressly includes "software": devices are "an instrument, apparatus, appliance, implant, material or other article, whether used alone or in combination, **including a software** or an accessory, intended by its manufacturer to be used specially for human beings or animals which does not achieve the primary intended action in or on human body… by any pharmacological or immunological or metabolic means, but which may assist in its intended function by such means for one or more of the specific purposes of (i) diagnosis, prevention, monitoring, treatment or alleviation of any disease or disorder…" [1]. CDSCO's MDSW guidance states it "applies to Software products which attract the definition of a 'Medical Device' as stipulated in the MDR-2017", categorised as SiMD or SaMD [1].

CDSCO released a **draft "Guidance Document on Medical Device Software" for stakeholder comments on 21 October 2025** [1][2][3]; trade sources report it has since been finalised after industry feedback [3][4] — the finalisation date/gazette reference is **UNKNOWN** (not verifiable from fetched primary sources as of this writing).

The guidance's own risk-factors table asks, for any software: "Whether the software impacts/influences or replaces any otherwise manual or clinician performed actions? [Examples: automated steps, **triages patients, provides a definite diagnosis or suggests likely diagnosis for further confirmation by physician, performs or recommends treatment**, identifies a region of interest for further review]" [1]. That describes AegisMed's core output. Independent regulatory-industry analyses reach the same conclusion: software intended for diagnosis of disease "qualifies as a medical device" under MDR 2017, and symptom-checkers are SaMD [2][27][37].

### 1.2 Risk classification (CDSCO Table 2 / IMDRF alignment)

CDSCO adopts the IMDRF-style two-axis table [1] (verbatim from the draft guidance):

| State of healthcare situation or condition | Treatment or diagnosis | Drive clinical management | Inform clinical management |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Critical** | D | C | B |
| **Serious** | C | B | A |
| **Non-serious** | B | A | A |

With the critical caveat (verbatim): "**Note: SaMD intended to be used by non-clinical users in a 'serious situation or condition' as described here, without the support from specialized professionals, may be considered as SaMD used in a 'critical situation or condition'. It may, hence, influence the risk classification of the SaMD.**" [1]

"Aid in diagnosis" is explicitly within "**Drive clinical management**": "the information provided by the SaMD shall be used to aid in treatment, aid in diagnoses, to triage or identify early signs of a disease or condition or/and will be used to guide next diagnostics or next treatment interventions" [1]. "Treatment or diagnosis" applies where the software will "Diagnose/screen/detect a disease or condition" [1].

**Assessment for AegisMed (consumer, unaccompanied by clinicians):**
- Output = ranked condition likelihoods from free-text symptoms → significance ≈ "Drive clinical management" (aid diagnosis/triage), or "Treatment or diagnosis" if the UI is framed as identifying the condition itself.
- User = consumer (non-clinical) → the Note can escalate "serious" to "critical".
- Therefore: **Class B minimum; Class C (or D) plausible** depending on intended-purpose wording and the seriousness of conditions covered. If AegisMed's list includes conditions whose mismanagement causes serious deterioration or death (stroke, sepsis, cardiac events), Class C–D arguments exist. Exact class must be fixed in the intended-purpose statement and, if unlisted, "they may seek clarification from the CLA regarding its risk classification" [1]. This is a legal judgement — **mark for Indian regulatory counsel**.

### 1.3 Registration pathway (Online System for Medical Devices / MD Online portal)

From the CDSCO guidance and MDR 2017 licensing structure [1][30][31]:

| Class | Authority | Form | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| A (non-sterile, non-measuring) | — | Registration under **Chapter IIIb** on MD online portal | Verbatim: "Class A (non-sterile and non-measuring) medical devices are exempted from the licensing requirements under MDR-2017, such medical device software shall be registered as per Chapter IIIb of MDR-2017 in the MD online portal." [1] |
| A (other) / B | **State Licensing Authority (SLA)** | Application **Form MD-3** → licence **Form MD-5** | QMS audit for Class B; fee ₹500/device + premise fee (₹5,000 Class A / ₹10,000 Class B) [30][31] |
| C / D | **Central Licensing Authority (CLA, CDSCO)** | Application → licence **Form MD-7** | CLA scrutiny; test licence Form MD-16 via NSWS portal for development/evaluation [1] |

Statutory processing timelines are set in the MDR 2017 ("The time line required for processing various license applications is mentioned in the MDR-2017" [1]); the commonly cited clock for an MD-5 manufacturing licence is **up to ~180 days** including query cycles [30][31] — industry-reported, not a verbatim rule quote.

### 1.4 Clinical evaluation requirements

The CDSCO guidance requires applicants to "ensure the determination of the **valid clinical association**/scientific validity of a Medical Device Software, demonstrating that it corresponds to the clinical situation, condition, indication or parameter defined in its intended purpose." Accepted data types (verbatim list): "Technical Standards, Literature searches; Professional medical society guidelines; Systematic scientific literature review; Clinical Investigation/Clinical performance studies; Published Clinical data; Secondary data analysis" [1]. Clinical-investigation permission (CL/Form MD-… under the Seventh Schedule) is required for investigational devices where a predicate does not exist in India [1]. Practical consequence: AegisMed's diagnostic accuracy evaluation (see docs 02/06) is the evidentiary backbone for any Indian submission; performance studies on Indian data strengthen the file.

### 1.5 Telemedicine Practice Guidelines 2020 — what AI may and may not do

Issued **25 March 2020** by MoHFW ("On March 25, 2020, the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, India, had released the 'Telemedicine Practice Guidelines'"), prepared by the Board of Governors superseding the MCI with NITI Aayog [7]. Primary text: MoHFW PDF [5] (site returned HTTP 403 to automated fetch; text quoted via Medindia's republication [6]).

- Prohibition (clause 2.8, as quoted by Medindia from the MoHFW text): "The guidelines **do not permit technology platforms based on Artificial Intelligence/Machine Learning to counsel the patients or prescribe** any medicines to a patient or a care giver. Only a Registered Medical Practitioner may counsel or prescribe…" [6]
- Corroborated verbatim by the *Indian Journal of Psychiatry* analysis: "The use of artificial intelligence or machine learning applications **to counsel or to prescribe medicines is strictly prohibited**." [7]
- Permitted role: AI/ML and data-science-based **decision support may assist and support an RMP**; final judgement, counselling and prescription must come from the human doctor [6][7][38].
- Liability: "Penalties and punishment for misconduct and negligence would be similar to an in-person consultation provided under the existing laws" [7]; platforms can be blacklisted for violations [38].

**Consequence for AegisMed:** as a standalone consumer product it must not be described as teleconsultation, counselling, or prescribing. If an RMP-escalation feature is added, the RMP — not AegisMed — must counsel and prescribe [5][6][7].

### 1.6 DPDP Act 2023 and DPDP Rules 2025 (health data, consent, children, penalties)

- **Act:** Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (Act No. 22 of 2023). Consent standard (s. 6, as analysed): "free, specific, informed, unconditional and unambiguous with a clear affirmative action" [8]; s. 8(5) requires "reasonable security safeguards" to prevent personal data breach [8][9].
- **Rules:** The **DPDP Rules, 2025 were notified in the Official Gazette on 13–14 November 2025** (draft was consulted in January 2025) [9][12][13]. Phasing: Data Protection Board rules immediate; **Consent Manager regime ≈ 13 November 2026**; **general compliance (notices, breach reporting, security safeguards, children's rules) ≈ 13 May 2027** [12][13]. Exact Gazette citation numbers **UNKNOWN** (verify against egazette.gov.in before relying on precise dates).
- **Health data:** the DPDP regime is consent-centric with **no special "sensitive category" tier**; health data of data principals is protected through the general consent, notice, purpose-limitation, security and breach-notification duties [8][9][12]. Rule 11/Schedule carve-outs reference healthcare professionals and educational settings for children's-data mechanics [8].
- **Children (s. 9 + Rule 10):** anyone under 18 is a child; processing requires **verifiable parental/guardian consent** (verification via "ID documents or a Digital Locker" [8]); tracking, behavioural monitoring and targeted advertising directed at children are restricted [9][10].
- **Security safeguards (Rule 6):** "encryption, obfuscation, masking, and the use of virtual tokens", access controls, backups [8]; **72-hour breach notification** to the Board [8][12]; erasure defaults around 3 years (Rule 8) [8].
- **Penalties (Schedule to s. 33):** security-safeguard failure **up to ₹250 crore**; children's-data obligations and breach-notification failure **up to ₹200 crore** each; Significant Data Fiduciary duties **up to ₹150 crore**; other violations **up to ₹50 crore**; data-principal duties ₹10,000 [9][10][11].

### 1.7 Advertising/claims law in India

**Drugs and Magic Remedies (Objectionable Advertisements) Act, 1954** (verbatim from the official Act text mirror [34]):
- s. 3(d) prohibits advertising referring to any drug "for… the **diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treatment or prevention of any disease, disorder or condition specified in the Schedule**" (Schedule includes blindness, cancer, cataract, diabetes, glaucoma, insanity, pneumonia, typhoid fever, ulcers of the gastro-intestinal tract, venereal diseases, etc.) [34].
- s. 4 prohibits any advertisement relating to a drug which "(a) directly or indirectly gives a false impression regarding the true character of the drug; or (b) makes a false claim for the drug; or (c) is otherwise false or misleading in any material particular" [34].
- s. 7 penalty: first conviction "imprisonment which may extend to **six months**, or with fine, or with both"; subsequent conviction "imprisonment which may extend to **one year**" [34].

A consumer app UI is "advertisement" territory when it markets capability. Marketing copy must avoid cure/treatment efficacy claims about scheduled diseases and avoid misleading claims about the system's accuracy [34]. (Whether software guidance is a "drug" advertisement is a contested question — the safe path is claims discipline. **WEAK-EVIDENCE** on application of DMRA to pure software; flagged, not asserted.)

## 2. United States (secondary market)

### 2.1 The statutory frame: FD&C Act §520(o)(1)(E) — verbatim

21 U.S.C. §360j(o)(1)(E) (21st Century Cures Act, 2016) excludes from the device definition any software function that meets **all** of the following (verbatim, public-domain text [16]):

> "(E) unless the function is intended to acquire, process, or analyze a medical image or a signal from an in vitro diagnostic device or a pattern or signal from a signal acquisition system, for the purpose of—
> (i) displaying, analyzing, or printing medical information about a patient or other medical information (such as peer-reviewed clinical studies and clinical practice guidelines);
> (ii) supporting or providing recommendations **to a health care professional** about prevention, diagnosis, or treatment of a disease or condition; and
> (iii) enabling such health care professional to independently review the basis for such recommendations that such software presents so that it is not the intent that such health care professional rely primarily on any of such recommendations to make a clinical diagnosis or treatment decision regarding an individual patient." [16]

Also relevant, §520(o)(1)(B): the device definition excludes software "for maintaining or encouraging a healthy lifestyle and is **unrelated to the diagnosis, cure, mitigation, prevention, or treatment of a disease or condition**" [16]. And §520(o)(3) lets the Secretary pull functions back into device status upon finding "serious adverse health consequences", considering "the intended user and user environment" and "whether there is a reasonable opportunity for a health care professional to review the basis" [16].

### 2.2 FDA guidance: Sept 2022 final → Jan 2026 revised final

- Final guidance "**Clinical Decision Support Software**" issued **28 September 2022**, significantly narrowing the 2019 draft (dropping the "internally consistent" reading, tightening criterion 4) [15][17][39].
- **A revised final guidance, dated January 2026 (current as of 01/29/2026 on fda.gov), supersedes the 2022 version** [14][15][40]. Per the EHRA analysis of the revision: it adds an enforcement-discretion policy for single-recommendation CDS, is technology-neutral between AI and non-AI CDS, and aligns "independent review" with human-in-the-loop safeguards [15].
- **"Time-critical decision-making":** the Cures Act term is not defined; FDA's guidance treats CDS intended for "critical or life-threatening conditions requiring action within 24 hours" (examples: sepsis, stroke) as time-critical and therefore *device* CDS, because a clinician cannot realistically independently review the basis in time [15][17][39]. (EHRA criticises the 24-hour reading as "vague and overbroad" [15].)
- FDA's September 2022 report to Congress stated additional legislative authority was needed for the large set of non-device-qualifying CDS [17][39].

### 2.3 Where a consumer symptom-checker falls

- Criterion (ii)–(iii) are **professional-facing**: the exclusion exists only where recommendations are made "to a health care professional" who can "independently review the basis." A consumer-facing symptom checker with no clinician in the loop cannot satisfy (ii)/(iii) [16][39][40]. FDA separately reaffirmed that device-CDS policies "apply to software functions that meet the definition of a device, including those that are intended for use by patients or caregivers" [14].
- Therefore a true consumer diagnostic-assistant is a **regulated device** in the US (typically 510(k) or De Novo, with the regulatory pathway driven by intended use/indications for use). Marketing it as a diagnostic tool without clearance risks adulteration/misbranding exposure [18][19].
- The alternative positioning is the **§520(o)(1)(B) healthy-lifestyle exclusion / 2019 General Wellness policy**: enforcement discretion for low-risk products that make **no disease-related claims** — the moment claims become diagnostic ("identifies what condition you may have"), the exclusion is lost [18][19][41].
- Enforcement pattern: FDA warning letters target unapproved/misbranded diagnostic claims, including AI/algorithm products told their outputs "only flag suspected findings and must not be used as a diagnostic tool" [18][19]. Symptom-checker-specific FDA warning letters were not located in this research — **UNKNOWN** (would require a full Warning Letters database trawl).

### 2.4 Practical US options for AegisMed

(a) Stay out of the US market; (b) launch a US version with strictly non-diagnostic, informational/wellness claims (no condition likelihoods presented as diagnosis, no disease-specific directives); or (c) pursue 510(k)/De Novo (costly; user fees published annually under MDUFA V; timeline typically 6–12 months post-submission — industry-reported; exact FY2026 fee figures **UNKNOWN** in this research).

## 3. European Union (secondary market)

### 3.1 MDR 2017/745 Rule 11 — verbatim (via MDCG 2021-24 Rev.1, which reproduces the rule)

> "**Rule 11 — Software intended to provide information to inform decisions with diagnosis or therapeutic purposes or software intended to monitor physiological processes.**
> Software intended to provide information which is used to take decisions with diagnosis or therapeutic purposes is classified as **class IIa**, except if such decisions have an impact that may cause:
> — death or an irreversible deterioration of a person's state of health, in which case it is in **class III**; or
> — a serious deterioration of a person's state of health or a surgical intervention, in which case it is classified as **class IIb**.
> Software intended to monitor physiological processes is classified as class IIa, except if it is intended for monitoring of vital physiological parameters, where the nature of variations of those parameters is such that it could result in immediate danger to the patient, in which case it is classified as class IIb.
> **All other software is classified as class I.**" [20]

MDCG 2021-24's classification example maps directly onto symptom checkers (verbatim): "**MDSW intended for diagnosing depression based on a score resulting from inputted data on patient symptoms (e.g. anxiety, sleep patterns, stress etc.)**" — **Class IIa** [20]. Note 2 (verbatim): "For the classification of software, it is needed to consider the intended purpose, intended population (including e.g. diseases to be treated and/or diagnosed), context of use (e.g. intensive care, emergency care, home use)… and of the possible decisions to be taken" [20]. Secondary analyses agree: most stand-alone diagnostic software is IIa or higher, i.e. **Notified-Body territory** [42][43].

**CE marking requirements for a Class IIa MDSW:** EU authorised representative if non-EU manufacturer; ISO 13485-certified QMS audited by the notified body; technical documentation (Annex II/III) incl. software lifecycle (IEC 62304), risk management (ISO 14971), usability (IEC 62304-adjacent IEC 62366), post-market surveillance plan; **clinical evaluation** under Annex XIV (literature-based route possible for well-characterised technologies) [20][28][29][43]. Published pricing does not exist — NB quotes vary; overall programs are commonly reported in the multi-hundred-thousand-euro range over 12–24 months (**industry-reported; no official price — UNKNOWN**). Related: MDCG 2025-4 (16 June 2025) governs safely making MDSW apps available on online platforms/app stores [44]; MDCG 2025-6 / AIB 2025-1 addresses the MDR–AI Act interplay [26].

### 3.2 EU AI Act — high-risk status and timeline

- Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (in force 1 August 2024): AI systems that are **safety components of products covered by Annex I harmonisation legislation — which includes MDR/IVDR devices — are high-risk under Article 6(1)**; MDR Class IIa+ AI software is therefore high-risk (MDR Class I AI is not, but still bears transparency duties) [25][26][45].
- **AI Omnibus (Digital Omnibus on AI): adopted as Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, entered into force 27 July 2026**, deferring Chapter III high-risk obligations: **Annex I (MDR-embedded AI) → 2 August 2028**; **Annex III → 2 December 2027** [24][25][26]. Prohibitions (as amended) phase in from 2 December 2026; Article 50 transparency obligations were **not** delayed (from 2 August 2026) [24][25].
- Net effect: an AegisMed EU launch as a CE-marked Class IIa device triggers MDR obligations immediately upon market placement and AI Act high-risk duties (risk management, data governance, technical documentation, logging, human oversight, post-market monitoring) by 2 August 2028 [24][25][26].

## 4. Cross-cutting

### 4.1 WHO guidance on AI in health

- **2021:** *Ethics and governance of artificial intelligence for health* (28 June 2021, ISBN 9789240029200) sets out "**six consensus principles**": protecting human autonomy; promoting human well-being, safety and the public interest; ensuring transparency, explainability and intelligibility; fostering responsibility and accountability; ensuring inclusiveness and equity; promoting AI that is responsive and sustainable [21].
- **2024:** *Ethics and governance of artificial intelligence for health: Guidance on large multi-modal models (LMMs)* — released **January 2024** (news item 18 January 2024; publication page metadata shows 25 March 2025 re-issue, ISBN 9789240084759) — issues 40+ recommendations to governments, developers and deployers: regulatory oversight and registration, human control over healthcare decisions, transparency, combating misinformation/bias [22][23].
- These are soft law but are the reference standard courts, regulators and ethics boards increasingly cite [21][22][23].

### 4.2 Liability for AI health advice

- No settled body of case law yet exists on consumer symptom-checker misdiagnosis liability; the frontier is *Raine v. OpenAI* (San Francisco Superior Court, CGC-25-628528, filed 26 August 2025): wrongful-death + **strict products liability** (chatbot as defective product) + negligence over ChatGPT's health/mental-health advice; OpenAI's Nov 2025 response invokes user misuse and ToS violations; California JCCP coordination of multiple chatbot product-liability suits is underway [35][36].
- Scholarly consensus: where algorithms operate without clinician approval, "liability shifts at least partially to the developer" (Cestonaro et al., 2023, heavily cited) [36][37]; disclaimers alone are a weak shield where the tool functions as a diagnostic product [37].
- India-specific: the Consumer Protection Act 2019 product-liability chapter and medical-negligence jurisprudence would govern; TPG 2020 equates telemedicine misconduct penalties with in-person consultation standards [7]. Detailed Indian case law on AI symptom-checkers: **UNKNOWN** (none located).
- Regulatory pushback on "wellness" positioning: FDA's General Wellness policy confines non-device status to products with **no disease claims** [41]; warning letters warn AI tools that outputs "only flag suspected findings and must not be used as a diagnostic tool" [18]; in the EU, MDCG 2019-11 qualifies individualised symptom evaluation as MDSW regardless of "wellness" branding, and market commentary notes a lawyer-written disclaimer "will not save" mispositioned software [43][46]. Babylon Health's symptom checker faced public safety complaints (2018–2021) including failure to flag heart-attack symptoms — an object lesson in reputation risk even without litigation [37].

### 4.3 App-store policies

- **Apple App Review Guidelines (fetched verbatim, current):**
  - "1.4.1 Medical apps that could provide inaccurate data or information, or that could be used for diagnosing or treating patients may be reviewed with greater scrutiny." — "Apps must clearly disclose data and methodology to support accuracy claims relating to health measurements, and if the level of accuracy or methodology cannot be validated, we will reject your app." — "Apps should remind users to check with a doctor in addition to using the app and before making medical decisions." — "If your medical app has received regulatory clearance, please submit a link to that documentation with your app." [32]
  - "1.4.2 Drug dosage calculators must come from the drug manufacturer, a hospital, university, health insurance company, pharmacy or other approved entity, or receive approval by the FDA or one of its international counterparts." [32]
  - Guideline 5.1.1(ix): healthcare services must be submitted by a legal entity, not an individual developer [32].
- **Google Play Health apps policy (fetched verbatim, current):** all developers must complete the **Health apps declaration** [33]; apps must "remind users to consult a healthcare professional for medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment"; "Non-medical-device health apps must include a clear disclaimer in their app description indicating that the app is not a medical device"; "Medical-device apps must provide proof of approval, clearance or certification by the relevant authority upon request"; "We don't allow apps containing misleading health claims that contradict existing medical consensus, or can cause harm to users" [33].

## 6. Implications for AegisMed

Concrete changes, mapped to the codebase:

1. **Reposition the UI language now (frontend/src).** Never label outputs "diagnosis". Use "informational symptom assessment — not a diagnosis; always consult a registered medical practitioner". Under CDSCO's guidance even "suggests likely diagnosis for further confirmation by physician" is device language [1]; under the FDA's Jan-2026 guidance and Apple 1.4.1, unvalidated accuracy claims get apps rejected [14][32]. Add a persistent disclaimer component on every results screen, and a one-tap "consult a doctor" escalation (Apple requires the reminder [32]; Google requires it too [33]).
2. **Never describe AegisMed as teleconsultation, counselling, or prescribing (India).** TPG 2020 clause 2.8 prohibits AI/ML platforms from counselling or prescribing [5][6][7]. If doctor-escalation is added, the RMP does all counselling/prescribing; AegisMed only hands over structured notes (FHIR already helps here).
3. **DPDP-compliant consent flow (frontend/src + backend).** Implement: itemised notice in plain language (Rule 3) listing purposes (symptom analysis, session storage, improvement), data collected, rights, grievance contact; consent capture as a logged, unambiguous affirmative action (s. 6); an **age gate** — if age <18, require verifiable parental consent per s. 9/Rule 10 and **disable any tracking/ads/analytics for child users**; retention + erasure request workflow (Rule 8 ~3-year default).
4. **Harden data handling in src/data/database (SQLite).** Current SQLite session store must be encrypted at rest (Rule 6 lists "encryption, obfuscation, masking" [8]) — e.g., SQLCipher or encrypted BLOBs; add access controls, tamper-evident audit logs, backup policy, and a **72-hour breach-notification runbook** to the DPB [8][12]. Minimise: store derived assessment records, not raw free-text symptoms, where possible.
5. **Classify before launch, not after.** Draft the intended-purpose statement, run it against CDSCO Table 2 [1], and decide: (a) claim Class A informational registration (only credible if output is genuinely non-diagnostic), or (b) budget for Class B/C licensing via MD Online (Form MD-3→MD-5) [1][30]. Do not launch a diagnostic claim unregistered; penalties and blacklist powers exist [7][30].
6. **Build the clinical-evidence file as a product artefact.** CDSCO requires valid clinical association evidence (literature, society guidelines, studies) [1]; MDR Annex XIV requires the same for CE; Apple 1.4.1 demands disclosed methodology for accuracy claims [32]. Turn docs/02 + docs/06 evaluations into a living Clinical Evaluation Report.
7. **What NOT to claim in the UI/marketing (India, DMRA 1954):** no cure, treatment, mitigation, or prevention claims for scheduled diseases (cancer, diabetes, glaucoma, blindness, typhoid, etc.), no "magic remedy"-adjacent promises, no false/misleading accuracy claims [34] — criminal exposure up to 1 year imprisonment [34].
8. **US strategy:** either geo-restrict the diagnostic features, or ship a claim-free informational version (s. 520(o)(1)(B) "unrelated to the diagnosis, cure, mitigation, prevention, or treatment of a disease or condition" [16]) — and keep the FHIR outputs of the US variant free of device-signal ingestion to preserve any future non-device CDS argument [16]. Do not enable the US variant to present likelihoods as condition identification.
9. **EU strategy:** treat CE-marking (Class IIa via notified body) as a separate, funded workstream, not a marketing checkbox — Ada needed audits, technical documentation and clinical evidence for IIa [28]; Infermedica's IIb was won "with zero non-conformities" after a rigorous clinical evaluation program [29]. If EU release is contemplated, start QMS (ISO 13485) and IEC 62304 processes early; AI Act high-risk duties land 2 Aug 2028, but Art. 50 transparency (disclosing AI-generated outputs) applies to EU users from Aug 2026 [24][25].
10. **App-store compliance as a release gate:** complete Google Play's Health apps declaration and add the "not a medical device" description disclaimer or regulatory proof, as applicable [33]; for Apple, submit accuracy methodology and any regulatory documentation, and keep 5.1.1(ix) in mind (legal entity, not individual developer) [32].
11. **Patent/provisional filing (IPO) hygiene:** claims and the specification should emphasise the **technical architecture** (multi-agent orchestration, uncertainty calibration, FHIR normalisation, escalation/safety logic) rather than promising "accurate diagnosis of diseases" — diagnostic-method framing both narrows patentability (computer-implemented medical methods) and creates admissions that the product is a regulated diagnostic device. Avoid stating regulatory status ("complies with CDSCO/FDA/MDR") in the patent or marketing until a determination exists.
